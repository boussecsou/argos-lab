# AI Drafts Still Need an Editorial Quality Gate

AI can shorten the path from a brief to a readable draft. It does not shorten the responsibility for publishing that draft. For a technical content lead, the operational question is not whether a model can produce fluent prose; it is whether the organization can show what was checked, what evidence supports the claims, and who accepted the remaining risk. An explicit editorial quality gate supplies that answer.

## What the sources establish

AI output should not be treated as a sole source of factual truth. OpenAI’s terms state that output may be inaccurate and require users to evaluate accuracy and appropriateness, including human review where appropriate, before sharing it. [OpenAI Terms of Use](https://openai.com/en-GB/policies/terms-of-use/) That is a provider-specific policy, but it captures a general editorial reality: polished language is not evidence.

Traceability matters for the same reason. NIST’s work on digital-content transparency examines ways to authenticate content and track its provenance, and its Generative AI profile notes that provenance tracking can help users assess trustworthiness. [NIST: Reducing Risks Posed by Synthetic Content](https://www.nist.gov/publications/reducing-risks-posed-synthetic-content-overview-technical-approaches-digital-content) [NIST Generative AI Profile](https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=958388) For an article, that does not require a novel provenance system. It does require a reader-independent record linking each material factual claim to a source, date, and verification status.

## Editorial recommendation

Treat generation as drafting and the quality gate as a release decision. The gate should test four distinct concerns. First, factual verification: check names, dates, numbers, quotations, product behavior, and causal claims against primary or otherwise appropriate sources. Second, source traceability: make sure every consequential assertion can be traced to a working source, not merely to a model prompt or an uncited research note. Third, structural completeness: confirm that the article delivers the promised audience, scope, explanation, limitations, and conclusion. Fourth, false confidence: identify sentences that sound certain while their evidence is thin, outdated, or absent.

False confidence is particularly easy to miss because a coherent draft can make unsupported transitions feel earned. The review should therefore ask a different question from “Does this read well?” Ask: “What would an expert challenge first, and can we show the support?” A source list at the bottom is insufficient if the reviewer cannot map the list back to individual claims.

Use this pre-publication checklist:

1. Mark every material factual claim and verify it against a cited source.
2. Prefer primary documentation, research, or first-party records; flag weaker sources.
3. Check that links work, quotations are accurate, and sources are current enough for the topic.
4. Compare the draft with the brief: reader, promised answer, required sections, and stated limitations.
5. Assign a named editor to resolve unsupported claims, overconfident wording, and open questions before publication.

This gate need not turn into a slow committee. A concise claim-to-source sheet and an accountable final reviewer are often enough for routine pieces. What matters is that the process can stop a fluent but unverified draft from being mistaken for finished editorial work.

The record also makes corrections cheaper: when a source changes, the team can find the affected claim instead of re-auditing the entire article.
